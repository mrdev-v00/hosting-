CHAPTER 1

Newly Emergent State — 1947–1958

The story of a new country building a state while dealing with partition, refugees, economic weakness, territorial disputes and constitutional development.

SLO 1.1.1 Analyse implementation of the basic components of the ideology of Pakistan

The syllabus names Islam, brotherhood, equality, justice and democracy. To “analyse” them, do more than define them: explain how they were reflected in the early state and where implementation faced difficulties.

Islam

Pakistan was created with a strong Islamic ideological identity. The Objectives Resolution (1949) declared that sovereignty belongs to Allah while authority is exercised by the people through their chosen representatives. The 1956 Constitution made Pakistan an Islamic Republic. Later constitutional development continued to combine Islamic principles with representative government and fundamental rights.

Brotherhood

Brotherhood meant a sense of common community among Muslims despite differences of language, region and social background. This principle mattered especially after Partition because the new state had to integrate migrants and different regional populations into one political community.

Equality

Equality was expressed through the idea that citizens should have equal status and protection before the law. Pakistan's constitutional development included fundamental-rights language covering equality and protection of citizens. In practice, however, social and economic inequalities did not disappear simply because equality was a constitutional principle.

Justice

Justice involved the rule of law, fair treatment and an independent judicial structure. Pakistan inherited legal institutions from British India and gradually developed its own constitutional framework. The 1973 Constitution, for example, explicitly protects equality before law, social/economic/political justice and judicial independence; these principles help explain the longer-term constitutional direction even though the SLO focuses on the early state.

Democracy

Pakistan initially operated under the Government of India Act 1935 as its interim constitutional framework. The 1956 Constitution introduced a parliamentary system, but political instability prevented the new democratic system from developing normally. Martial Law in 1958 then interrupted the parliamentary experiment.

Exam method: For “analyse implementation,” use principle → early constitutional/institutional evidence → practical difficulty → judgement about the extent of implementation.
SLO 1.1.2 Analyse the significance of the Two-Nation Theory in the establishment of Pakistan

The Two-Nation Theory argued that Muslims and Hindus of British India constituted distinct political communities or nations because of major differences in religion, culture, social practices, traditions and historical identity. It did not mean that Muslims and Hindus had never lived together; they had shared the subcontinent for centuries.

Its political significance was that it gave the Pakistan Movement an argument for separate political safeguards and, eventually, a separate homeland. Muslim League leaders argued that a permanent Hindu-majority political system could leave Muslims vulnerable to majoritarian rule. The Lahore Resolution of 1940 gave the movement a decisive constitutional-political direction toward Muslim-majority regions being organized as separate political units.

After the 1946 elections and the failure of constitutional negotiations to produce an arrangement acceptable to the major political forces, the demand for Pakistan became the basis of the 1947 transfer of power. Thus, in an exam, connect the theory to Muslim political identity → demand for safeguards → Pakistan Movement → establishment of Pakistan.

Precision: Avoid writing “Muslims and Hindus could not live on the same land.” The historical argument was about political nationhood and representation, not a claim that coexistence was physically impossible.
SLO 1.1.3 Discuss the immediate problems faced by Pakistan after independence

(a) Economic problems — industry and agriculture

Pakistan began with a relatively weak industrial base compared with the economy of undivided British India. Many major industrial and commercial facilities were located in areas that became India. Pakistan also had to create government institutions, support refugees, maintain defence and finance development at the same time.

Agriculture was central to the economy, especially in Punjab and Sindh, but Partition disrupted markets and supply chains. Jute and cotton are good examples of the problem: agricultural production was divided from some of the processing industries that had historically served it. Pakistan therefore had to develop domestic industry and new trade relationships.

(b) Unjustified / disputed division of resources — financial and military

The assets and liabilities of British India had to be divided between the new dominions. Pakistan's share of cash balances and other assets became the subject of dispute and delay. The military division was also difficult because many ordnance factories, training institutions, workshops and stores were located in India. Pakistan consequently began with shortages in equipment, infrastructure and industrial support for defence.

Fact-check against common notes: Do not memorize an unsupported “$950m versus $900m” story. Standard historical accounts discuss Pakistan's agreed share of cash balances in rupees and the withholding of part of that share for a period. Use the figures in your textbook if your teacher expects a specific textbook figure.

(c) Administrative problems — capital and government

Pakistan had to build a central administration almost immediately. It needed ministries, departments, courts, financial institutions, police, communications and a functioning chain of government. Karachi became the first capital and housed the central government while the state developed its institutions.

Constitution-making was another major challenge. The Objectives Resolution was adopted in 1949, but political disagreement delayed a permanent constitution. The 1956 Constitution finally came into force on 23 March 1956, only to be abrogated when Martial Law was imposed in October 1958.

(d) Rehabilitation of refugees

Partition caused a vast movement of people. Millions of Muslims moved into Pakistan, many arriving without their homes, businesses or financial resources. The new government had to provide camps, food, medical assistance, housing and employment, and then move refugees toward longer-term settlement.

This was not simply a humanitarian issue; it was also an administrative and economic challenge. The government had to spend scarce resources while simultaneously building the new state. Refugees also contributed skills, professional experience and entrepreneurship to Pakistan's cities and economy.

(e) Problem of national language

Language became connected with national identity and political representation. Urdu was promoted as a common national language, while Bengali speakers in East Pakistan argued that Bengali deserved official recognition because Bengali speakers formed the majority of Pakistan's population.

The dispute intensified into the Bengali Language Movement, particularly in 1952. The issue showed that national unity could not be built only by choosing a language; regional identities and questions of representation also mattered.

Important correction: East Pakistan did not separate during 1947–1958. It remained part of Pakistan until 1971. Keep the language dispute in this chapter separate from the later 1971 separation.

(f) Security threats — India and Afghanistan

Relations with India were difficult from the beginning, especially because of Kashmir and other unresolved Partition issues. Pakistan also had difficult relations with Afghanistan over the status of the frontier and the Durand Line. These concerns forced Pakistan to devote scarce resources to defence and foreign policy while it was still establishing its economy and administration.

These threats were not simply solved by closing a border or by one early treaty. They remained part of Pakistan's security and foreign-policy environment.

(g) Accession of princely states — Kashmir, Junagadh and Hyderabad

At Partition, princely states had to determine their future political relationship. The three cases in your syllabus are useful because each had a different combination of ruler, population and accession decision.

(h) Canal water dispute — causes and impacts

Partition divided the irrigation system of Punjab. Important canal headworks were on the Indian side while canals supplied agricultural areas in Pakistan. In 1948, water supplies to some Pakistani canals were interrupted, producing a serious dispute because Pakistan depended heavily on irrigation.

The dispute eventually moved toward negotiations supported by the World Bank. The Indus Waters Treaty was signed in 1960: the Western Rivers (Indus, Jhelum and Chenab) were allocated principally to Pakistan and the Eastern Rivers (Ravi, Beas and Sutlej) principally to India, subject to the treaty's detailed rules.

SLO 1.1.4 Assess the extent to which Pakistan successfully overcame its initial challenges

“Assess” requires balance. Pakistan did succeed in establishing a functioning state despite starting with major shortages. It created central institutions, rehabilitated large numbers of refugees, developed an administrative system, built armed forces and eventually produced a constitution in 1956.

However, several challenges remained unresolved or only partly addressed. Kashmir remained disputed; language and regional tensions continued; economic and administrative capacity remained limited; and the parliamentary system was interrupted by Martial Law in 1958.

Strong conclusion structure: “Pakistan overcame some immediate survival and institution-building problems, but political, territorial, economic and regional issues remained. Therefore, the extent of success was substantial in state-building but incomplete in resolving deeper structural disputes.”
CHAPTER 2

Political Developments Under Different Regimes

1958–2007 and political developments from 2007 onward.

2.1 General Ayub Khan's Era — 1958–1969

2.1.1 Reasons for Martial Law and Ayub Khan coming to power

The 1956 Constitution created a parliamentary system, but political instability continued. There were frequent changes of government and disagreements between political institutions. President Iskander Mirza concluded that the political system was failing to provide stability. On 7 October 1958, he abrogated the Constitution, dissolved the assemblies and imposed Martial Law, appointing General Ayub Khan as Chief Martial Law Administrator.

On 27 October 1958, Ayub removed Iskander Mirza and assumed the presidency. Thus, Ayub's rise was the result of the military becoming the central political institution after the collapse of the 1956 parliamentary arrangement. Ayub's supporters presented military rule as a way to restore stability and administrative effectiveness; opponents regarded it as an interruption of constitutional democracy.

2.1.2 Strengths and weaknesses of Basic Democracies (BDO)

The Basic Democracies Order of 1959 created a system of local councils designed to involve citizens in government at the grassroots. The local representatives, known as Basic Democrats, became an electoral college for the president and assemblies.

Potential strengths: it created elected local institutions, encouraged participation below the national level, and linked local government to development work. It also provided a political structure after the suspension of ordinary party politics.

Weaknesses/criticisms: national political participation was indirect, political parties were restricted during the early Martial Law period, and the system concentrated substantial authority in the presidency. Because Basic Democrats formed the electoral college for the president, critics argued that the system did not amount to full parliamentary democracy.

Evaluation formula: “It increased local participation, but its indirect national structure and concentration of power limited the extent to which it functioned as competitive parliamentary democracy.”
2.1.3 Agricultural and industrial reforms introduced by Ayub Khan

Agricultural and land reforms

Ayub's government tried to increase agricultural productivity and reduce the power of very large landholdings. Land reforms introduced ceilings on individual holdings. The government also promoted mechanization, irrigation, improved seeds, fertilizers and agricultural credit. Development institutions and agricultural banks helped provide financing and inputs.

The broader agricultural modernization associated with the 1960s is often called the Green Revolution. High-yield varieties, irrigation, fertilizer and mechanization increased yields, although access to these inputs was uneven and larger farmers often benefited more easily.

Industrial reforms

The government encouraged private industrial investment through tax incentives, credit, development finance and export incentives. Industries such as textiles, cement, engineering and other manufacturing expanded. Foreign aid and investment also supported development.

Ayub's economic strategy was therefore not simply “build factories”; it was a wider programme of planned development + private investment + agricultural modernization + export promotion.

Other reforms worth knowing

The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 changed rules concerning marriage, divorce, maintenance and polygamy procedures. Ayub's government also invested in education, health, infrastructure and family planning. These are useful supporting examples even though the syllabus focuses especially on agriculture and industry.

2.1.4 Impact of Ayub Khan's reforms on the economy

Pakistan experienced strong economic growth during the 1960s. Industrial output increased, agricultural productivity improved in many areas, infrastructure expanded and development planning became more systematic. The period is therefore often associated with rapid modernization.

However, growth and equality are not the same thing. Critics pointed to concentration of wealth and unequal regional benefits. East Pakistan in particular argued that economic growth and development resources were not distributed fairly. The economic model also depended heavily on foreign assistance and private investment.

For “impact,” separate growth from distribution: growth in production and infrastructure can be acknowledged while also discussing inequality, regional disparities and dependence on external support.
2.1.5 Impact of the 1965 war on socio-economic and political conditions

The 1965 India–Pakistan war placed additional pressure on government resources and disrupted the development environment. Defence needs increased, while the political atmosphere became more sensitive.

The war ended with a ceasefire and the Tashkent Declaration (1966). The agreement became politically controversial inside Pakistan, and opposition figures used it to criticize Ayub's handling of the war and its aftermath.

In the years after 1965, opposition grew, including criticism of economic inequality, political restrictions and regional grievances. The war was therefore one factor in a wider process of political weakening rather than a single cause of Ayub's fall.

2.1.6 Reasons behind the fall of Ayub Khan's government

By 1968–69, opposition to Ayub had become widespread. Political parties demanded greater democratic participation, students and workers protested, and dissatisfaction existed over economic inequality and regional disparities. Opposition in East Pakistan was especially significant.

Ayub's government increasingly struggled to contain the unrest. In March 1969, he resigned and transferred power to General Yahya Khan, who imposed Martial Law. The fall therefore resulted from a combination of political opposition + mass unrest + regional grievances + economic inequality + declining legitimacy.

2.2 Yahya Khan's Era — 1969–1971

2.2.1 Legal Framework Order (LFO) and its relationship with East–West Pakistan

Yahya Khan took power in 1969 and promised elections and a return to representative government. The Legal Framework Order (1970) established the rules for the elections and principles for the future constitutional system. It also ended the One Unit arrangement in West Pakistan, restoring the provinces.

Most importantly for East–West relations, representation in the National Assembly was based on population. East Pakistan therefore received a majority of the National Assembly's general seats. This was politically significant because East Pakistan had the larger population and could, in principle, form the central government through electoral majority.

2.2.2 Reasons for tension between East and West Pakistan

Political representation: East Pakistanis argued that their population majority had not translated into equivalent political power under earlier arrangements such as parity between the two wings.

Administration: Many major institutions and decision-making structures were concentrated in West Pakistan. East Pakistanis wanted greater control over decisions affecting their region.

Economic distribution: East Pakistan produced major export earnings, especially from jute, yet many East Pakistanis believed government expenditure and development investment favored West Pakistan.

Armed forces: Bengalis were underrepresented in senior military and security institutions, reinforcing the perception of political and institutional inequality.

These were not isolated complaints; together they created a powerful regional autonomy movement.

2.2.3 The Six Points of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman

The Six-Point Programme, announced by Sheikh Mujibur Rahman in 1966, demanded a highly decentralized federation. Its core purpose was to give East Pakistan much greater control over its political and economic affairs.

  1. Federal structure and parliamentary government: the federation would be based on the spirit of the Lahore Resolution, with a parliamentary system and a legislature elected on adult franchise.
  2. Limited federal subjects: the central government would mainly handle defence and foreign affairs; remaining subjects would belong to the provinces.
  3. Currency: either two separate but freely convertible currencies, or one currency with strong constitutional safeguards to prevent capital flight from East to West.
  4. Taxation: provinces would have the main power to levy and collect taxes, with the federation receiving a share for its limited responsibilities.
  5. Foreign exchange: separate accounts would record the foreign-exchange earnings of the two wings, with provinces exercising control over their own earnings and trade.
  6. Security: East Pakistan would have its own militia/paramilitary force.

Supporters saw the Six Points as a programme for provincial autonomy; opponents feared that such extensive autonomy would make the central state too weak.

2.2.4 Major events leading to the separation of East Pakistan

1970 elections

The December 1970 general election was Pakistan's first general election based on one-person-one-vote. The Awami League won an overwhelming majority of seats in East Pakistan and, because East Pakistan had more seats, it won a majority in the National Assembly overall.

Failure to transfer power / dispute over the Six Points

The election result created a constitutional crisis. Negotiations among Yahya Khan, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto did not produce an agreed transfer of power. The dispute centered partly on the extent of provincial autonomy and the future constitutional structure.

1971 conflict and war with India

The political crisis became an armed conflict in East Pakistan. India became directly involved, and the conflict developed into the India–Pakistan War of December 1971. Pakistani forces in East Pakistan surrendered, and Bangladesh emerged as an independent state.

Do not reduce 1971 to one cause. A strong answer connects long-term political/economic/military grievances with the 1970 election, failure to transfer power, internal conflict and India's intervention.

2.3 Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's Era — 1971–1977

2.3.1 Reasons behind Bhutto's coming to power

After the 1971 war and the surrender of Pakistani forces in East Pakistan, Yahya Khan resigned. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto assumed the presidency and became the leading political figure in the remaining Pakistan.

Bhutto's Pakistan Peoples Party had strong support in West Pakistan, and the country needed political reconstruction after the trauma of 1971. Bhutto later became Prime Minister under the 1973 Constitution.

2.3.2 Bhutto's reforms — land reforms and nationalisation — and their impacts

Land reforms

Bhutto's government attempted to reduce the concentration of agricultural land by imposing ownership ceilings and redistributing some land. The aim was to weaken very large estates and improve the position of smaller cultivators and tenants.

The impact was mixed. Some land was redistributed, but large landowners remained politically and economically influential, and implementation varied across regions. The reforms therefore reduced some holdings without completely transforming the rural power structure.

Nationalisation

The government brought major parts of industry, banking and other sectors under state ownership. The stated purpose was to reduce concentration of economic power and place strategic industries under public control.

Nationalisation expanded the role of the state in the economy. Supporters associated it with redistribution and public control; critics argued that bureaucratic management and uncertainty about private investment could reduce efficiency and discourage investment. The result was therefore a major structural change rather than a simple success-or-failure story.

Related constitutional reform

The 1973 Constitution restored a parliamentary framework and remains the central constitutional document of Pakistan. Its preamble combines democracy, equality, social justice and Islamic principles, while also recognizing fundamental rights and judicial independence.

2.3.3 Simla Agreement and its impact on Pakistan

The Simla Agreement (3 July 1972) was signed by Zulfikar Ali Bhutto and Indian Prime Minister Indira Gandhi after the 1971 war. The agreement committed India and Pakistan to peaceful relations and to resolving differences through peaceful means, particularly bilateral negotiations.

It helped establish a framework for post-war relations and was connected with the return of prisoners of war and the normalization process. The agreement also provided for the Line of Control in Jammu and Kashmir to be respected pending a final settlement.

Analysis: Explain both the immediate post-war purpose and the longer-term diplomatic framework it created.

2.4 General Zia-ul-Haq's Era — 1977–1988

2.4.1 Reasons behind Zia-ul-Haq taking over, with reference to the 1977 elections

In the March 1977 general election, Bhutto's Pakistan Peoples Party won a large majority. The opposition alliance, the Pakistan National Alliance, alleged widespread rigging and launched protests. The political crisis became severe, and negotiations between the government and opposition did not restore stability.

On 5 July 1977, General Zia-ul-Haq overthrew Bhutto's government and imposed Martial Law. Zia initially described the intervention as temporary and promised elections, but military rule continued for many years.

2.4.2 Zia's reforms — Islamization and privatization — and their impact

Islamization

Zia's government pursued state-led Islamization in law, courts, taxation, education and public life. Major measures included the Hudood Ordinances, the Zakat and Ushr Ordinance, the creation of the Federal Shariat Court, changes to banking practices toward profit-and-loss arrangements, and stronger Islamic content in education and public institutions.

The Eighth Amendment later became important in the constitutional structure of the period, including changes that strengthened the presidency. Zia also established the Majlis-e-Shoora and held a non-party election in 1985.

Impacts were contested. Supporters viewed Islamization as bringing state institutions closer to Islamic principles; critics argued that some laws and institutions created new legal, social and gender-related difficulties. In an exam, describe the measure first and then explain the documented impact or criticism rather than using broad labels.

Privatization

Zia's economic direction moved away from Bhutto-era nationalisation and toward greater private-sector participation. The policy encouraged private ownership and investment, although the full privatization programme continued and developed further in the later civilian and Musharraf periods.

2.5 Restoration of Civilian Rule — 1988–1999

2.5.1 Political changes between 1988 and 1999

After Zia-ul-Haq died in August 1988, Pakistan returned to elected civilian government. Benazir Bhutto became Prime Minister after the 1988 election. Her government was dismissed in 1990, after which Nawaz Sharif became Prime Minister. Benazir returned to office in 1993, was dismissed in 1996, and Nawaz Sharif returned in 1997. His government was removed in the 1999 military coup.

The period therefore shows both restoration of civilian rule and continuing instability. Presidents, prime ministers, courts, political parties and the military all played important roles in the political system.

2.5.2 Benazir Bhutto's reforms — women empowerment and rural electrification

Benazir Bhutto's governments promoted greater participation of women in public institutions and supported policies associated with women's access to employment, education and public services. The significance for the syllabus is that these policies aimed to increase women's role in national life rather than treating them as outside the political and economic system.

Rural electrification aimed to extend electricity infrastructure to villages and rural communities. Electricity could support household use, education, irrigation, small businesses and rural services. The impact should be discussed in terms of improved infrastructure and access, while recognizing that implementation depended on resources, power-sector capacity and geographic coverage.

2.5.3 Nawaz Sharif's reforms — transport development and nuclear experiment

Transport development

Nawaz Sharif's governments emphasized infrastructure and road development. The Islamabad–Lahore Motorway became a major symbol of this approach, improving high-speed road connectivity and representing a shift toward large-scale infrastructure projects.

Nuclear tests

After India conducted nuclear tests in May 1998, Pakistan conducted its own nuclear tests at Chagai on 28 and 30 May 1998. The tests had major security, diplomatic and economic implications, including international sanctions and a new phase in South Asian strategic relations.

2.6 General Pervez Musharraf's Era — 1999–2007

2.6.1 Reasons behind the 1999 military coup

Relations between Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif and Army Chief General Pervez Musharraf deteriorated sharply after the Kargil conflict of 1999. On 12 October 1999, while Musharraf was returning to Pakistan, Nawaz Sharif attempted to remove him as army chief. The military took control of key state institutions and Musharraf became the country's military ruler.

For an exam, distinguish the immediate trigger — the attempt to remove Musharraf — from the wider background of political and civil-military tensions.

2.6.2 Musharraf's economic reforms — FDI, value-added production and privatization

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

The government tried to attract foreign investors by improving the investment environment, liberalizing sectors and encouraging private investment. FDI can bring capital, technology, management expertise and access to international markets.

Privatization

The government restructured and privatized public enterprises in areas including banking, telecommunications, energy and other sectors. The stated economic logic was to reduce the burden of inefficient state enterprises, improve management and encourage competition and private investment.

Value-added production

Value-added production means moving beyond the export of raw materials toward processing and manufacturing products with greater economic value. For Pakistan, the goal was to increase industrial productivity, exports, employment and foreign-exchange earnings.

Pakistan experienced a period of relatively strong economic growth in the early 2000s. However, growth should not automatically be treated as proof that every reform worked equally well; distribution, sustainability, external conditions and the performance of particular sectors must also be considered.

2.7.2 Political developments aimed at strengthening democracy

The period after 2007 included a return to civilian government in 2008, followed by parliamentary elections and transfers of power between elected governments. A major constitutional development was the 18th Amendment (2010), which strengthened Parliament and increased provincial autonomy while changing the distribution of powers between the President, Prime Minister and provinces.

The significance of these developments is that they moved the constitutional structure toward a stronger parliamentary and federal model. At the same time, Pakistan continued to experience political conflict, institutional tensions and changes of government, so the SLO expects discussion of developments rather than a simple claim that democracy was either “perfect” or “failed.”

2.7.3 Assess implications of FDI, privatization and industrialization in the current scenario

FDI: Foreign investment can provide capital, technology, management expertise and links to international markets. Its limitations include dependence on investor confidence, exchange-rate conditions, political stability and the ability to retain enough value inside the domestic economy.

Privatization: Selling or restructuring state-owned enterprises can reduce government financial burdens and introduce private management and competition. However, the outcome depends on regulation, market competition, transparency, pricing and the quality of the enterprise being privatized. Concerns can also arise about employment and access to services.

Industrialization: Expanding manufacturing can create jobs, increase exports, develop skills and diversify the economy. But industrial growth requires reliable energy, transport, finance, skilled labour, technology, environmental management and stable economic policy.

Assessment structure: define the policy → explain intended benefit → explain possible limitation → connect it to Pakistan's present economic needs → give a balanced conclusion supported by evidence.

EXAM TOOLKIT

How to answer the SLO verbs

The wording in the syllabus tells you what the examiner expects.

DiscussExplain the topic from relevant angles. Include causes, features, actions and effects rather than a one-line definition.
AnalyseBreak the issue into parts and explain relationships: cause → action → effect → significance.
EvaluatePresent strengths/benefits and weaknesses/limitations, then make a reasoned judgement supported by evidence.
AssessWeigh the evidence and judge the extent of success or impact. Avoid absolute claims when the evidence is mixed.
ExplainState what something was and make the mechanism or reason clear. If it is a reform, explain purpose + implementation + effect.
ListGive the required names/events accurately, preferably with dates when useful.
Universal Pakistan Studies paragraph: Context → Cause → Event/Reform → Immediate effect → Longer-term impact → Balanced assessment.
